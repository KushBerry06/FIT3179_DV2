What Makes Australia’s Top Dog?

About half of Australian households have a dog. But which breeds do Australians actually choose, where did those breeds come from, and what are they like to live with?

HOW TO READ THIS PAGE

By “top dog” we mean the breeds most registered with Dogs Australia, the national pedigree registry.

Crossbreeds and unregistered dogs are not counted, so this is the pedigree story, not every dog in Australia.

Scroll down to follow the story in five steps.

01 · A NATION OF DOG OWNERS

Dogs are Australia’s favourite pet, but the pedigree scene is shrinking

Almost one in every two Australian households has a dog.

Dogs are comfortably Australia’s most common household pet. In 2025, 49.3% of households reported owning a dog, compared with 33.6% for cats.

Fish appear in 11.5% of households and birds in 9.5%, while small mammals and reptiles each appear in fewer than 3%.

Dogs are present in almost half of Australian homes.

Dog ownership is widespread, but pedigree participation is not.

The map uses Dogs Australia members per 100,000 residents rather than raw membership, allowing states with different population sizes to be compared fairly.

Tasmania records the highest rate at 151.6 members per 100,000 people, followed by South Australia at 139.3. The Northern Territory records the lowest rate at 55.7.

Population size does not necessarily mean stronger pedigree participation.

7 of 8 jurisdictions recorded fewer members in 2025 than in 2021.

ACT membership grew 43.4%, from 309 to 443 members.

Dog ownership remains widespread, while pedigree participation is shrinking.

Australians love dogs, but most registered favourites were developed overseas. Where did they come from?

02 · WHERE OUR DOGS COME FROM

Our top dogs are immigrants

Dog breeds were developed all over the world, but Europe stands out.

The proportional-symbol map shows how many recognised FCI breeds are associated with each country. Larger circles represent countries linked with more breeds.

The United Kingdom, France and Germany form one of the strongest concentrations, while recognised breeds also originate across Asia, the Americas, Africa and Oceania.

Europe is a major centre of recognised breed development.

5 of 10 selected breeds trace back to the United Kingdom.

UK-origin breeds account for more than two-thirds of registrations among these ten breeds.

Australia’s favourites may be registered locally, but their origins are overwhelmingly overseas.

Where a breed came from is also linked to what it was bred to do.

The chord diagram connects broad origin regions with recognised FCI breed groups.

Thicker connections represent more recognised breeds linking a region with a particular breed group. This reveals how different parts of the world developed different mixtures of working, hunting and companion dogs.

Geography tells us where breeds began. Breed groups reveal the roles they were developed for.

Origins explain where these breeds began. Registrations show which ones Australians choose today. Which breeds now lead the pack?

03 · THE POPULARITY CONTEST

Golden Retrievers became number one by falling more slowly

Australia’s pedigree market has become much smaller.

Dogs Australia recorded 77,559 registrations in 2000. By 2025 the total had fallen to 47,288, about 39% lower.

Registrations briefly climbed to 78,580 in 2021, before falling sharply over the following four years.

A breed can climb the rankings even while its own registration numbers are falling.

Before individual breeds, one group dominates the registry.

Breed groups provide a broader view of what Australians register. In 2025, Gundogs accounted for 26.0% of all Dogs Australia registrations.

Working Dogs followed at 18.3%, with Terriers, Toys and Utility dogs also representing substantial shares.

Gundogs alone account for more than one quarter of all 2025 registrations.

The Golden Retriever finished 2025 ranked first among the ten selected breeds.

It overtook the Labrador Retriever in 2024.

Rank is relative. A breed can rise because other breeds fall faster.

Golden Retriever registrations fell 15.9% from 2000 to 2025.

Labrador registrations fell 31.0% over the same period.

Only the Australian Shepherd (+326.7%, from 281 to 1,199) and French Bulldog (+232.5%, from 203 to 675) grew.

Registration data tells us what Australians choose. It does not tell us what those dogs are like to live with.

04 · LIVING WITH THEM

Popularity doesn’t tell you what a dog is like to live with

Most selected breeds score very highly for affection.

Trainability, dog friendliness and barking vary much more between breeds.

Darker means a higher score, not necessarily a better trait.

The same dog can rank high on one characteristic and low on another.

The parallel-coordinates chart compares height, weight, lifespan and general health. Each measure is normalised within its own metric so very different units can be compared together.

Choose a breed from the dropdown or hover over a line to isolate it from the rest.

There is no single profile that defines the “best” dog.

Across these breeds, heavier dogs tend to live shorter lives.

Great Danes and Bernese Mountain Dogs sit near the heavy, shorter-lived end.

This is a breed-level pattern, not a prediction for an individual dog.

Ownership, origins, popularity and breed characteristics tell different parts of the story. So which breed is really Australia’s top dog?

05 · VERDICT

The top dog depends on how you count

Among the ten breeds followed here, the Golden Retriever finishes 2025 on top. But it got there because Labrador registrations fell faster, not because Golden Retriever registrations grew.

Australia’s most familiar pedigree breeds are overwhelmingly imported, many of them from the United Kingdom. Popularity also tells us surprisingly little about daily life: breeds differ substantially in temperament, body size and lifespan.

Popularity tells us what Australians register. It cannot tell us which dog is right for a particular home.
